Step 1

Begin with the axes

Before looking at a line or a point, ask two questions: What is being measured? and In what units? The axis labels provide the answers.

Meaning first: A number such as 4 has no clear meaning by itself. It might mean 4 dollars, 4 hours, or 4 meals. A graph becomes readable when each axis names both the variable and its unit.

Vertical axis

Step 4

Intercepts describe edge cases

An intercept is where a graph meets an axis. At that location, the variable measured on the other axis equals zero.

Meaning: An intercept answers an edge-case question: “What does the model predict when one variable is zero?”

Ice cream

At zero degrees Celsius, predicted sales equal 10 per hour.

The vertical intercept (0, 10) says that predicted sales are 10 per hour when temperature is 0°C.

Hot chocolate

At zero degrees sales equal 120; at 30 degrees sales equal zero.

The intercepts (0, 120) and (30, 0) describe sales at zero temperature and the temperature at zero sales.

Model boundary: A line can be extended mathematically, but its predictions may stop being realistic. Interpret an intercept within the context and useful range of the model.

Step 5

Direction describes a relationship

Direction tells whether the variables move together or in opposite directions.

Ice cream: 40/hour · Hot chocolate: 60/hour

Positive relationship

Ice-cream sales rise with temperature.

As temperature rises, predicted ice-cream sales rise.

Negative relationship

Hot-chocolate sales fall with temperature.

As temperature rises, predicted hot-chocolate sales fall.

Use the words carefully: “Positive” and “negative” describe movement, not whether something is good or bad. A relationship alone does not prove causation.
Step 6

Slope is a rate of change

Choose any two points on a straight line. Measure the vertical change, measure the horizontal change, and divide. The result is the same everywhere on that line.

Start with the movement: The symbol Δ (the Greek letter delta) means “change in.” Therefore, Δx means “change in x,” and Δy means “change in y.” slope = Δy ÷ Δx Always move from the first point to the second in the same direction when calculating both changes.

Positive constant slope: ice-cream sales

The line rises from left to right, so its slope is positive. The two graphs use different pairs of points on the same line.

Pair 1: A to B

The first point pair gives a slope of positive two.
  1. Horizontal change: ΔT = 15 − 5 = 10°C.
  2. Vertical change: ΔI = 40 − 20 = +20 per hour.
  3. slope = +20 ÷ 10 = +2

Pair 2: C to D

A different point pair gives the same slope of positive two.
  1. Horizontal change: ΔT = 25 − 15 = 10°C.
  2. Vertical change: ΔI = 60 − 40 = +20 per hour.
  3. slope = +20 ÷ 10 = +2

What stayed constant? The triangles are in different locations, but both have a rise of 20 and a run of 10. Every 1°C increase is associated with 2 additional ice creams sold per hour.

Negative constant slope: hot-chocolate sales

The line falls from left to right, so its slope is negative. Again, two different pairs of points produce the same result.

Pair 1: A to B

The first point pair gives a slope of negative four.
  1. Horizontal change: ΔT = 15 − 5 = 10°C.
  2. Vertical change: ΔH = 60 − 100 = −40 per hour.
  3. slope = −40 ÷ 10 = −4

Pair 2: C to D

A different point pair gives the same slope of negative four.
  1. Horizontal change: ΔT = 25 − 15 = 10°C.
  2. Vertical change: ΔH = 20 − 60 = −40 per hour.
  3. slope = −40 ÷ 10 = −4
Why the slope is constant: A straight line has the same steepness everywhere. Changing the two selected points can change the sizes of Δy and Δx, but their ratio remains unchanged. On a curve, the steepness—and therefore the slope—can change.

Check the idea

Two points farther apart on the same straight line produce a rise of 40 and a run of 20. What should happen to the slope?

Step 8

Explain the whole graph

Imagine that someone gives you a graph and asks, “What can you tell from it?” Start by finding out what the graph is about. Then use its points, intercepts, slope, and equation to tell the full story.

A useful reading order: What do the axes measure? What does one point mean? Where does the line begin? Does it rise or fall? How fast does it change? What does the equation say? Finally, what does all of this mean in the real world?

Story 1: Ice-cream sales

What are we talking about? The horizontal axis shows outdoor temperature in degrees Celsius. The vertical axis shows how many ice creams the shop sells per hour. So this graph is about the relationship between temperature and ice-cream sales.

Ice-cream sales rise with temperature.

What can we learn from the graph? The line begins at (0, 10). This means the model predicts 10 ice creams sold per hour when the temperature is 0°C. The point (20, 50) gives another part of the story: at 20°C, the model predicts 50 sales per hour.

As we move to the right, the line rises. Temperature and ice-cream sales have a positive relationship. The slope is +2, so each 1°C increase is linked to 2 more ice creams sold per hour.

The equation I = 10 + 2T says the same thing in a shorter way: start with 10 sales and add 2 for every additional degree.

The whole story: In this model, warmer weather is linked to higher ice-cream sales. The graph does not prove that temperature is the only cause of sales, but it shows a clear positive relationship.

Story 2: Hot-chocolate sales

What are we talking about? The horizontal axis again shows outdoor temperature in degrees Celsius. This time, the vertical axis shows cups of hot chocolate sold per hour. The graph is about the relationship between temperature and hot-chocolate sales.

Hot-chocolate sales fall with temperature.

What can we learn from the graph? The line begins at (0, 120). This means the model predicts 120 cups sold per hour when the temperature is 0°C. The point (20, 40) means that at 20°C, the model predicts 40 cups sold per hour. The other intercept, (30, 0), means predicted sales reach zero at 30°C.

As we move to the right, the line falls. Temperature and hot-chocolate sales have a negative relationship. The slope is −4, so each 1°C increase is linked to 4 fewer cups sold per hour.

The equation H = 120 − 4T tells us to start with 120 sales and subtract 4 for every additional degree.

The whole story: In this model, warmer weather is linked to lower hot-chocolate sales. Temperature is not the only thing that affects sales, but the graph shows a clear negative relationship.

Step 9

Calculate areas from linear graphs

A region above or below a straight line may form a right triangle. Read the base and height as differences between coordinates, then apply the triangle formula.

Area of a right triangle: ½ × base × height. Base and height are lengths, so subtract their endpoint values first.

Area below a line

The triangle has base 4 and height 7.

Base: 4 − 0 = 4
Height: 12 − 5 = 7

½ × 4 × 7 = 14

Area above a line

The triangle has base 5 and height 8.

Base: 5 − 0 = 5
Height: 11 − 3 = 8

½ × 5 × 8 = 20

Check the method

Step 10

Curves can be concave or convex

First ask: does the curve rise or fall? Then ask: does it become steeper or flatter?

Slope on a curve: The green line touches the curve at one point. It shows how steep the curve is there. A curve can have a different slope at each point, so its slope is not constant.

Concave: diminishing increases

Real-world story

At first, warmer weather may bring a large jump in ice-cream sales. Later, the shop may be close to capacity. Sales still rise, but by smaller amounts.

These graphs show the same curve at two temperature levels.

Lower temperature

Ice-cream sales have a relatively steep positive slope at the lower temperature.

slope ≈ +3.5 Sales rise quickly.

Higher temperature

The same ice-cream sales curve has a smaller positive slope at the higher temperature.

slope ≈ +0.4 Sales still rise, but slowly.

Why is it concave? Both slopes are positive, so sales rise. But +0.4 is smaller than +3.5, so the curve becomes flatter. This is called diminishing increases: sales keep increasing, but each equal temperature increase adds less than the one before.

Convex: diminishing decreases

Real-world story

At first, warmer weather may cause a large drop in hot-chocolate sales. Later, sales are already low. They still fall, but by smaller amounts.

These graphs show the same curve at two temperature levels.

Lower temperature

Hot-chocolate sales have a relatively steep negative slope at the lower temperature.

slope ≈ −3.5 Sales fall quickly.

Higher temperature

The same hot-chocolate sales curve has a negative slope closer to zero at the higher temperature.

slope ≈ −0.4 Sales still fall, but slowly.

Why is it convex? Both slopes are negative, so sales fall. But −0.4 is closer to zero than −3.5, so the curve becomes flatter. This is called diminishing decreases: sales keep decreasing, but each equal temperature increase subtracts less than the one before.

Remember: A straight line has the same slope everywhere. A curve does not. Diminishing increases means rising by smaller amounts. Diminishing decreases means falling by smaller amounts.

Check the real-world meaning

Hot-chocolate sales fall by 30 units after one temperature increase but by only 5 units after the same-sized increase at a higher temperature. What does this suggest?
